What a DIY builder actually costs

Wix and Squarespace advertise plans from roughly $15 to $45 a month, and that's a fair price for the software. What doesn't show up on the pricing page is the time. Building a site that reads well, loads fast, and actually converts calls takes most people 15–30 hours if they've never done it before — copywriting, layout, mobile testing, connecting a domain, setting up basic SEO. For a tradie running a business full-time, that's a weekend or three you're not getting back, and the result still needs someone with design sense behind it to look right.

What a freelancer typically charges

Freelance web designers in Australia commonly quote somewhere between $800 and $3,000 for a small business site, depending on experience and scope. The upfront number can look attractive next to an agency quote, but check what happens after handover. Most freelance arrangements don't include ongoing hosting, security patching, or ongoing content updates — that's usually a separate conversation, and a separate invoice, six months down the track when you need to change your phone number or add a new service.

What an agency typically charges

Traditional agencies building a custom small business site usually start around $3,000 and can run past $8,000 once you add copywriting, photography, and a content management system. Some bundle in a monthly maintenance retainer on top of the build fee — worth asking about upfront, because it changes the real first-year cost significantly. Agency pricing generally reflects a bigger team and a bigger process, which is genuinely useful for a business with complex needs, but it's often more than a five-page trade business site requires.

What most quotes leave out

A website quote rarely covers everything you'll actually need in the first year. Here's what tends to show up as a separate cost later, whichever route you go:

  • Hosting — $120–$600 a year on a properly managed host, more if you're on a cheap shared plan that struggles under load.
  • Domain renewal — $20–$30 a year, easy to forget until it lapses.
  • Security and backups — $200–$800 a year if you want a managed solution rather than doing it yourself.
  • Copywriting — $400–$2,000 if it's not part of the build, and a site with weak copy won't convert regardless of how it looks.
  • Ongoing edits — commonly billed at $80–$200 an hour if you go back to a freelancer or agency after handover.

None of this makes the upfront quote dishonest — it's just rarely itemised. Worth asking for the full first-year number, not just the build price, before you compare options.

The real question to ask

Sticker price tells you almost nothing on its own. Ask what happens in month three when you need to add a new service area, or month eight when the site needs a security update. Ask who owns the domain. Ask what "unlimited updates" actually means in practice. Those answers matter more than the number on the invoice.

Questions worth asking

Frequently asked questions

Is a free website build too good to be true?+
Not if the business is upfront about how it makes money. A free build funded by an ongoing monthly plan is a legitimate model — the business gets paid once you're happy enough to go live, which lines up its incentives with yours. Check for lock-in contracts and hidden setup fees either way.
What's the cheapest way to get a website for a trade business?+
DIY builders are cheapest in dollar terms, but factor in your own time realistically. If your time is worth more spent on jobs, a low monthly subscription with the build included often works out cheaper overall.
Do I need to pay a designer, or can I use a template?+
Templates work fine as a starting point. The gap that usually shows is copywriting written for your specific trade and area, and layout decisions that actually match how people search for tradies locally.
Are there ongoing costs after the website is built?+
Yes, almost always — hosting, a domain renewal, and security updates are ongoing regardless of who builds the site. The question is whether they're bundled into one fee or billed separately as they come up.
